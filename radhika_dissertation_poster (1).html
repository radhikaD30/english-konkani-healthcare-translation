A
Abstract
Language barriers in healthcare affect patient safety, particularly for speakers of low-resource languages. Konkani, spoken by ~7.6 million people across Goa and the Konkan coast, has no domain-specific MT tools for healthcare. This paper presents the first systematic study of English-to-Konkani machine translation in the health domain — benchmarking three commercial systems, evaluating the IndicTrans2 base model, and fine-tuning it on ~19,794 parallel ILCI health sentences to achieve BLEU 21.85 and chrF 54.67, a gain of +12.05 BLEU over the untuned baseline.
1
Introduction
Accurate healthcare communication is vital. In multilingual India, patients speaking low-resource languages often receive healthcare information through translation tools built for general text, with no medical adaptation. Konkani (ISO 639-3: gom) is an Indo-Aryan language spoken by ~7.6 million people in Goa, coastal Maharashtra, and Karnataka — one of India's 22 scheduled languages and the official language of Goa.

Healthcare text demands more than general text: drug names, anatomical terms, and clinical procedures must be translated precisely. Mistranslations carry real clinical risk. This paper addresses:
  • How well do current English–Konkani MT systems perform on health text?
  • What does IndicTrans2 achieve without fine-tuning (direct baseline)?
  • How much does domain-specific fine-tuning improve results?
2
Key Contributions
  • Benchmarking of 3 commercial English–Konkani MT systems on 992 health-domain sentences (BLEU, chrF, TER)
  • Evaluation of IndicTrans2 base model on 30-sentence health test set without fine-tuning → BLEU 9.80
  • First fine-tuned neural MT system for English–Konkani healthcare: BLEU 21.85, chrF 54.67
  • Multi-stage preprocessing pipeline (NER masking, MWE joining, verb chunking, Unicode normalisation)

Methodology

Preprocessing Pipeline (6 stages):
1
Unicode normalisation of Devanagari characters for consistent encoding
2
Contraction expansion on English side (e.g. don't → do not)
3
NER entity masking — drug names, dosages & medical abbreviations replaced with placeholders (__ENT5__) to prevent hallucination
4
MWE joining — collocations like blood_pressure, heart_disease joined as single tokens
5
Verb chunking — TAM constructs like should_be_taking joined to prevent segmentation errors
6
Length filtering — max 128 tokens after IndicTrans2 tokenisation

Evaluation Metrics
BLEU — Corpus-level n-gram precision (SacreBLEU, 13a tokenisation). ↑ Higher = better.
chrF — Character n-gram F-score; better for morphologically rich Devanagari script. ↑ Higher = better.
TER — Translation Edit Rate; word-level edits to convert hypothesis → reference. ↓ Lower = better.
R
Related Work
MT for Healthcare: Prior studies show commercial MT handles informal text adequately but produces errors in clinical instructions. The WMT Biomedical Shared Task benchmarks European languages; comparable Indian-language work remains sparse.

MT for Indian Languages: IndicTrans2 (Gala et al., 2023) covers all 22 scheduled Indian languages using IndicCorp v2, achieving state-of-the-art on IN22. Samanantar assembled large parallel corpora for 11 Indic pairs, though Konkani received less coverage.

English–Konkani NLP: Kangle et al. (2016) proposed an early rule-based system; Patil & Naik (2018) extended it with a hybrid approach. No prior study addresses domain-specific healthcare MT for this language pair.

8
Conclusion & Limitations
Fine-tuning IndicTrans2 on ~19,794 ILCI health sentence pairs achieves BLEU 21.85 and chrF 54.67 — a +12.05 BLEU gain over the base model and far surpassing all commercial systems. The fine-tuned model produces fluent Konkani with more accurate medical terminology and more natural verb forms.

Limitations: Training corpus is from a single source (ILCI), limiting sub-domain coverage (oncology, psychiatry). Evaluation uses single reference per sentence. Entity masking may fail for compound expressions not in the lexicon. Human evaluation by qualified Konkani annotators remains a future goal.
